# Blog Post Outline Generator

## Outline Templates

Choose the structure that matches the post’s purpose. Use one template as the primary framework and borrow elements from another only when they improve clarity.

### Template Selection Guide

| Template | Best Used For | Recommended Structure | Example Title |
|---|---|---|---|
| Listicle | Posts covering tools, mistakes, ideas, or steps | Introduction → Selection criteria → Numbered items → Summary → CTA | Seven Practical Ways to Beat the Blank Page |
| How-to | Posts teaching a specific outcome | Promise → Prerequisites → Steps → Validation → Common mistakes → CTA | How to Turn a Blank Page into a Blog Post Outline |
| Problem-Solution | Posts helping readers diagnose and fix an issue | Problem → Consequences → Root causes → Solution → Implementation → Prevention | Why Blog Posts Stall—and How to Restart Them |
| Opinion or Analysis | Posts presenting a position or examining a trend | Thesis → Context → Supporting arguments → Counterargument → Implications → Recommendation | Why a Working Outline Matters More Than a Perfect First Draft |

### Reusable Outline Framework

| Outline Beat | What to Include | Example: “How to Turn a Blank Page into a Blog Post Outline” |
|---|---|---|
| Working title | Make the topic, reader, and outcome clear | How to Turn a Blank Page into a Blog Post Outline |
| Audience | Identify whom the post serves | Bloggers who regularly struggle to begin |
| Reader problem | Describe the frustration in familiar language | The page is open, but ideas feel disconnected or incomplete |
| Promise | State what the reader will accomplish | Build a focused outline before drafting the article |
| Hook | Open with a relatable moment, question, or observation | A blinking cursor can make a simple writing task feel unusually difficult. |
| Context | Explain why the problem occurs and why it matters | Strong posts need a clear progression from one idea to the next. |
| Main point one | Present the first actionable principle | Start with the reader and the outcome the post will deliver. |
| Main point two | Present the second actionable principle | Choose a structure based on the post’s purpose. |
| Main point three | Present the third actionable principle | Give each section one job and a clear transition to the next. |
| Example | Show the principle applied to a realistic situation | A how-to post might move from prerequisites to steps, validation, and mistakes. |
| Objection | Handle a likely hesitation | The outline does not need to contain every sentence. |
| Conclusion | Restate the outcome and reinforce the next step | A complete outline turns an intimidating page into manageable sections. |
| Call to action | Ask for one relevant action | Draft one section using the outline and review the next step. |

## Writing Prompts

Use these prompts in order to move from a blank page to a structured, publishable draft. Replace the bracketed variables with topic-specific details, and never invent statistics or quotations—mark claims that require verification.

### 1. Define the Blog Post

**Foundational prompt**

> Help me plan a blog post about **[working title]** for **[target reader]**. Their main problem is **[problem]**. After reading, they should be able to **[desired outcome]**. Suggest a clear thesis, five to eight relevant subtopics, a search intent, and a specific next step. Flag any claims that need supporting evidence.

| Brief Element | Guiding Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Working title | What specific outcome does the post deliver? | Turn One Blog Idea Into a 12-Post Content System |
| Target reader | Who needs this information? | Solo bloggers with inconsistent publishing schedules |
| Problem | What is creating the blank-page block? | Starting every article without a repeatable process |
| Promise | What will the reader gain or accomplish? | A repeatable workflow for developing one article into a month of content |
| Thesis | What is the post’s central argument? | A strong content system starts with one useful blog post, not twelve disconnected ideas |
| Search intent | What does the reader expect to learn or do? | Learn how to repurpose a core blog post into a content plan |
| Evidence | What examples or sources will strengthen the argument? | A sample 12-post calendar and reader-question examples |
| Call to action | What should the reader do next? | Select one idea and complete the workflow today |

### 2. Generate Titles and Angles

**Angle prompt**

> Generate five distinct title-and-angle combinations for **[topic]**. Use a mixture of practical, curiosity-driven, contrarian, and benefit-led approaches. Avoid clickbait, keep every title under 65 characters, and explain the unique value of each angle.

| Title | Angle | Primary Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| How to Create a 12-Post Plan From One Blog Idea | Practical | Provides a repeatable process |
| Your Blog Does Not Need 12 New Ideas | Contrarian | Reframes the content-planning problem |
| One Article, Four Weeks of Content | Benefit-led | Emphasizes time and effort saved |
| The Content System I Use Before Opening a Blank Document | Personal/proof-led | Builds trust through method |
| What to Write Next: A Simple Repurposing Workflow | Search-led | Matches informational intent |

### 3. Build the Complete Outline

**Outline prompt**

> Create a detailed outline for **[selected title]** targeting **[reader]** at approximately **[word count]** words. Include an introduction, scannable H2/H3 headings, examples, transitions, objections, conclusion, and call to action. For every section, state its purpose, main points, evidence needed, estimated word count, and transition to the next section.

**Example outline: “Turn One Blog Idea Into a 12-Post Content System”**

| Section | Purpose | Main Content | Evidence or Example | Words |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| Hook | Establish the problem quickly | Twelve ideas do not solve inconsistent publishing | Open with the cost of rebuilding research every week | 80 |
| The reusable-content principle | Explain the core concept | One substantial idea can become multiple formats | Compare one article with a 12-post calendar | 120 |
| Step 1: Define the pillar topic | Show how to establish direction | Reader, problem, promise, and outcome | Use “overcoming blank-page block” as the example pillar | 140 |
| Step 2: Extract individual lessons | Turn the article into smaller posts | Group lessons by problem, process, mistake, and result | List six lessons from the pillar article | 150 |
| Step 3: Adapt each lesson | Explain format changes | Convert tutorials into checklists, FAQs, examples, and opinion posts | Demonstrate two lesson-to-post conversions | 200 |
| Build the calendar | Connect ideas to a schedule | Prioritize, sequence, and assign publication dates | Show a four-week sample calendar | 220 |
| Worked example | Prove the process is manageable | Move from one article to twelve planned posts | Map each post to its source section | 150 |
| Objection handling | Remove barriers to adoption | Address time, repetition, and audience fatigue | Explain how each repurposed post adds a distinct angle | 90 |
| Conclusion and CTA | Reinforce the next action | Select one idea and extract its lessons today | Invite the reader to draft the first three posts | 50 |
| **Total** | **Complete publishable structure** | **Coherent progression from problem to action** | **Research and examples identified before drafting** | **1,200** |

## Examples

The examples below are illustrative and show how the **Blog Post Outline Generator** can turn an unstructured idea into a publication-ready plan.

### Example 1: “How to Build a Sustainable Weekly Meal-Prep Routine”

**Initial brief**

| Brief field | Example input |
|---|---|
| Working topic | Weekly meal preparation for busy people |
| Target reader | Home cooks with limited weekday time |
| Reader problem | They plan meals but abandon the routine after an exhausting week |
| Blog objective | Help readers create a realistic preparation and storage system |
| Search intent | Informational and action-oriented |
| Preferred tone | Practical, encouraging, and concise |
| Personal angle | Lessons learned from maintaining a four-session weekly routine |
| Call to action | Invite readers to choose one prep day and schedule it before leaving the article |

**Generated outline**

| Section | Heading | Key points and suggested length |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | “Meal Prep Should Make Life Easier—not Harder” | Introduce the blank-page problem, explain the promise of a sustainable routine, and preview the four-step system. **120 words** |
| Context | “Why One-Time Meal Prep Often Fails” | Cover overplanning, unfamiliar ingredients, unrealistic schedules, and poor storage choices. **160 words** |
| Step 1 | “Choose a Schedule You Can Repeat” | Suggest selecting one preparation window, limiting the weekly menu, and keeping one flexible meal. **200 words** |
| Step 2 | “Build a Short, Repeatable Shopping List” | Organize staples, proteins, produce, freezer items, and a final “flex meal” component. **220 words** |
| Step 3 | “Use Prep Sessions for Specific Jobs” | Divide preparation into chopping, cooking, portioning, labeling, and cleaning tasks with a simple timeline. **280 words** |
| Step 4 | “Store, Reheat, and Recover Quickly” | Explain safe storage, reheating options, ingredient substitutions, and how to prevent food waste. **240 words** |
| Troubleshooting | “When the Routine Needs to Change” | Address missed prep days, schedule conflicts, disliked foods, and leftovers that will not last. **190 words** |
| Conclusion | “Start Smaller Than You Think” | Recap the four steps, encourage one-week testing, and repeat the call to action. **190 words** |

**Pre-publication checklist**

| Check | Complete example |
|---|---|
| Reader and problem are explicit | ✓ The article targets busy home cooks struggling to maintain meal prep |
| Headings follow a logical progression | ✓ Context → schedule → shopping → preparation → storage → troubleshooting |
| Every section has a defined purpose | ✓ Each heading includes actions, considerations, or examples |
| Intro and conclusion support the main promise | ✓ Both frame the routine as realistic and repeatable |
| Call to action is specific | ✓ Readers are asked to schedule one preparation session |
| Estimated length is realistic | ✓ The outline totals approximately 1,600 words |
